A note before we start

A scenario, not a prophecy

This essay borrows the format of AI 2027: a dated story, written to be argued with. Facts come from the sources listed at the end. Projections come from a small model whose assumptions you can inspect and change, and the panel beside the text redraws as you scroll and as you move the sliders.

The argument is balanced. AI will make Bhutan's greatest physical asset, cheap hydropower, more valuable than ever. It will also weaken the route that educated young Bhutanese would otherwise take into service jobs. Gelephu Mindfulness City (GMC) sits where those forces meet, and the outcome depends less on technology than on a few budget decisions between now and 2029.

October 2026

A small economy with one big asset

Bhutan has fewer than 800,000 people and an economy of roughly US$3.8 billion. It is growing fast: the World Bank projects 7.1 percent for 2025/26 and 6.4 percent the year after, driven mostly by new hydropower.[1][2] Tourism is recovering, with 181,516 arrivals in 2025, a quarter more than the year before.[3] Hydropower supplied around 2.5 gigawatts in 2024.[4]

The weak spot is people. Youth unemployment stands near 20.6 percent on the World Bank's April 2026 reading, though other series put it nearer 17 percent, and roughly nine percent of Bhutanese now live abroad.[1] Growth has not yet become enough work that young graduates want.

Into this comes GMC. The King announced it in December 2023, and a royal charter established it a year later as a Special Administrative Region of about 2,500 square kilometres in Sarpang, on the Indian border.[6] Its stated purpose is a hub for conscious, sustainable business rooted in Bhutan's Buddhist heritage. It has its own executive, legislative and judicial powers, a master plan by Bjarke Ingels Group and a governor in former Prime Minister Lotay Tshering.[7] More than 16,000 volunteers gathered there in February 2026.[8] What does not yet exist is the city itself.

2026 to 2027

Power becomes the product

AI has changed what electricity is worth. Large models need dense, reliable, low-carbon power, and many countries cannot supply it quickly. India, the nearest big market, is one of them: data centre growth there is held back by power availability and slow grid expansion.[9] Bhutan, with an almost entirely renewable river-fed grid, has the opposite problem: power, and little to do with it except export it.

The first moves have happened. Bitdeer opened a Bitcoin mining facility at Jigmeling in 2025, planned at 500 megawatts.[10] In July 2026 the Canadian firm SATO signed a letter of intent with the GMC Authority for a hydro-powered AI data centre campus, starting near 5 megawatts, with reports of 100 megawatts or more later.[11][12] A letter of intent is not a contract, and SATO was still talking to lenders when it announced the plan.[13]

Funding is just as unusual. In December 2025 the government pledged up to 10,000 Bitcoin to GMC, but holdings had already fallen from roughly 13,000 to about 5,400 by March 2026, so the pledge holds only if mining keeps refilling the reserve.[14] GMC's early construction is, in effect, paid for by selling hydropower in digital form.

2027 to 2028

The arithmetic problem

Here is the first honest complication. A data centre running at 100 megawatts all year uses about 876 gigawatt-hours. At a few US cents per kilowatt-hour, an assumption, that is tens of millions of dollars. The model uses US$0.35 million per megawatt each year, so 100 megawatts earns about US$35 million, under one percent of GDP, and 500 megawatts about US$175 million, a little over three percent. Real money, but not a transformation.

The jobs arithmetic is harsher. A data centre needs a few engineers, electricians and security staff, not thousands of workers. The model assumes 0.6 operating jobs per megawatt, so 150 megawatts supports about 90 permanent jobs. Compute is a good source of revenue and a poor source of employment.

Risks add up. Run-of-river hydropower is seasonal, an AI customer wants power every hour, chips are governed by export rules written elsewhere, and most of Bhutan's trade runs through India, also the likeliest customer. None of this kills the plan. All of it argues against betting on a single engine.

2028 to 2030

Four ways AI reaches the economy

Economists usually trace AI's effects through four channels: productivity, tasks, wages and structure. Each plays out differently in a small, hydropower-rich economy, and the model draws on all four.

Productivity. Better tools raise output per worker. About 42 percent of employed Bhutanese still work in agriculture, so modest gains from crop advice, weather forecasts or market information could matter more to household incomes than any server hall.[16] Tourism and public services could gain too. These gains depend on adoption and arrive slowly, so the model leaves them out of its headline numbers.

Tasks. AI replaces tasks, not whole jobs, and it handles routine cognitive ones best: customer support, data entry, bookkeeping, basic coding. Developing economies have often climbed from farming into exactly this work. If that ladder weakens as Bhutan's graduates reach it, the effect lands on a group already struggling. In the model this is the "AI squeeze", worth up to four points of youth unemployment by 2032.

Wages. Workers who use AI well may earn more while entry-level pay comes under pressure. Emigration is Bhutan's wage safety valve: it rose sharply after the pandemic among young, skilled people and civil servants, and Australia is among the destinations.[15] A worker who can earn more abroad has little reason to wait for a local job.

Structure. Compute turns electricity into an export, shifting weight toward energy and capital and away from labour. The arithmetic problem above is this channel at work: more revenue, few jobs.

Why 2032? Data centres take years to finance and build, while AI capabilities beyond about 2030 are too uncertain to forecast with discipline. Six years is long enough for GMC's first phases to show results, and short enough to keep the assumptions honest.

2029

The decision nobody announces

Around 2029, GMC faces a choice that will not be presented as one. It will arrive as budget lines: how much land and power to commit to compute, what share of proceeds to put into schools, training and research, and how hard to work at bringing Bhutanese talent home.

Three stylised strategies follow. Compute first goes up to 500 megawatts with little reinvested. Balanced build adds moderate compute and reinvests a quarter. People first limits compute, reinvests more than half and treats talent retention as the main product. Pick one or set the sliders yourself. The world is the same in each case; only the choices differ.

2030

Policy levers and what they cost

Each strategy in the model is a bundle of policies, and each policy has a price. Four matter most.

  • A reinvestment rule. Fixing a share of compute and Bitcoin proceeds for skills protects it from short-term spending, but removes flexibility when prices fall. Training without local jobs at the end of it can also produce better-qualified emigrants, an effect the model does not capture.
  • Local hiring conditions on data centre licences. These spread the benefits at home, but can deter investors who want speed, and invite token hiring that satisfies the rule without building skills.
  • Treasury rules for Bitcoin. A rule for how much to sell, and when, gives GMC predictable funds. Without one, spending follows the price, and sales in a downturn lock in losses.
  • Legal independence. GMC can write its own rules for data, AI use and visas faster than a national parliament. The trade-off is credibility, since investors will price the quality of its courts and regulators. Higher pay and different rules inside GMC could also pull workers away from the rest of Bhutan.

The model treats these as dials, but someone must turn them. Each trade-off is cheapest to settle in writing before the money arrives.

2030 to 2032

Baseline, upside and downside

By 2032 the paths have separated. These three scenarios use the model's presets. The card matching your settings is outlined, and the table compares them.

Baseline: the uneven middle Your settings

The most likely outcome, in my judgement. GMC attracts jobs in construction, hospitality, finance and education alongside a moderate compute campus. Youth unemployment settles around 16.6 percent. Some talent returns and much still leaves. It is neither triumph nor failure, and the choices of 2029 can still improve it.

Upside: the mindful hub Your settings

GMC ends up smaller in compute and larger in human capacity. Revenue is modest, about US$28 million a year, yet reinvestment in training, research and returnee incentives pulls youth unemployment toward 14.6 percent and holds emigration near 10 percent. Mindfulness becomes a product: trusted, human-centred education, care and governance in a world full of synthetic output.

Downside: powered but empty Your settings

The data centres run at full load and the revenue is real, but few Bhutanese work in them. Youth unemployment improves only to roughly 18 percent, and the share living abroad climbs above 11 percent. GMC has buildings and a balance sheet, but its identity as a mindfulness city is mostly a slogan.

Method

How this was made

The projections come from a transparent scenario model, not a trained forecasting system: seven yearly steps, five levers and a short list of assumptions, shown below and in the repository. Starting values come from the sources at the end; the coefficients are my judgement and are labelled that way.

AI tools helped in three places: web search to gather sources, an AI assistant to draft code and text, and the model itself as a way of testing arguments. Sources found this way need checking against the originals, and the repository records what still does.

The limits matter. There are no feedback loops, climate or political shocks, or unpredicted AI breakthroughs, and the link from skills to emigration runs one way, though training may sometimes help people leave. Treat the results as arguments in numerical form, not estimates.

What to watch

What would change this forecast

A forecast is useful only if it says what would prove it wrong. I would track these signals:

  • Letters of intent becoming financed contracts, and megawatts actually energised.
  • The share of data centre staff who are Bhutanese.
  • Bitcoin holdings against the 10,000-coin pledge.
  • Quarterly youth unemployment and emigration figures.

Slow energisation, mostly imported staff and rising emigration would push me toward the downside. Local hiring, a working research institute and returning graduates would push me toward the upside.

Bhutan does not need to choose between selling electricity and building people. It does need to decide, early and in writing, how much of the first will pay for the second.
